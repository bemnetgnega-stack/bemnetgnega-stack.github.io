Discovery Project: USB-C Fast-Charging Car Port with CarPlay In Progress

ECE 1100 Individual Discovery Project · Fall 2026

The Problem

My 2019 Toyota Camry has a factory USB-A port that supports wired Apple CarPlay but charges a modern phone very slowly. With navigation and music running, my phone can barely hold its charge on a long drive. Aftermarket USB-C chargers that plug into the 12 V socket charge quickly, but they don't carry data, so they can't run CarPlay. I wanted one port that does both: fast USB-C charging and full CarPlay data, in a module that fits where the factory port was.

The Process

I started by researching how phone charging actually works. Older USB-A ports signal charging current over the same D+/D− data lines that CarPlay needs, which is why a port can't easily be both a fast charger and a data connection. USB-C solves this with dedicated configuration (CC) pins that use the USB Power Delivery protocol to negotiate higher power on a separate channel. That leaves the data lines free for CarPlay.

Next, I got the same style of OEM USB module from a 2025 Toyota RAV4 so I could study its pinout and connector. Then I planned a breadboard prototype. A bench supply will stand in for the car's 12 V system, feeding an automotive-grade USB-C Power Delivery controller with a built-in buck-boost converter, such as TI's TPS25762-Q1, which accepts the 12 V input directly. The USB data lines pass straight through to the head unit. I also designed protection for the two biggest hazards in a car's electrical system: voltage dips during engine cranking and voltage spikes from load dump. I narrowed the design to a single port to keep the prototype focused and testable.

Key Design Decisions

  • One port, not two. I first wanted two USB-C ports, but I narrowed it to a single port so the prototype stays focused and testable.
  • Buck-boost instead of a fixed 5 V converter. A simple 5 V step-down converter limits USB-C to about 15 W. Real fast charging needs the higher voltages USB Power Delivery can request, so the controller has to adjust its output voltage, not just drop 12 V to 5 V.
  • Automotive-grade parts. A car's interior gets extremely hot, so I chose a controller rated to AEC-Q100 Grade 1, the automotive standard for parts that operate up to 125 °C.
  • Breakout board for prototyping. The controller comes on an evaluation board, so I can prototype on a breadboard without soldering tiny surface-mount parts.

The project is currently in the prototyping stage. My test plan checks two things inside the car: that the phone negotiates a higher charging current than the factory port provides, and that CarPlay connects and stays stable through the new port. I'll update this section with measured charging results, photos of the prototype, and what I'd change in a final version before the Discovery Project Showcase.

This project fits right into my focus on electric energy systems and circuit technology. It also taught me to read datasheets and reference designs critically, and to check my own assumptions. More than once, my first mental model of how the power and data paths connect turned out to be wrong, and correcting it made the design better.

Six-Wheel Agricultural Rover

Independent Project · Spring–Summer 2026 · Built and tested at CATLab

The Problem

Agricultural fieldwork like soil testing usually means walking equipment out to every spot by hand. I wanted to build a mobile platform that could carry that equipment across a field instead. I designed a six-wheel, remote-controlled rover with a modular frame that can carry different tools. My design targets were a 35 lb payload and a five-hour runtime. I led the rover build and collaborated with Ayman, who developed a soil colorimeter as an example of the kind of equipment the rover could carry. We presented the two projects together.

The Process

My work focused on the rover's electronics, controls, and prototyping. An Arduino Mega 2560 reads commands from a FlySky FS-iA6B radio receiver. Three TB6612FNG motor drivers run six 12 V DC gear motors, one per wheel. A PCA9685 servo board controls four steering servos, which give the rover four-wheel steering for tighter turns in crop rows. The frame is built from aluminum T-slot extrusion with 3D-printed parts and flexible TPU tires.

I wired all six drive motors and four steering servos, worked on the Arduino control code, calibrated the steering, and troubleshot the motor drivers. For testing, I powered the rover from a bench supply at about 11.1 V with a 5 A current limit, so a wiring mistake couldn't damage the parts. The onboard power plan uses two 3-cell LiPo packs in parallel, with a buck converter stepping the voltage down for the lower-voltage electronics.

The Hardest Problem

The wheels kept slipping on the motor shafts, so torque from the motor wasn't reaching the ground. I redesigned the connection as a removable 3D-printed adapter held by a set screw. My first revision failed, so I revised the design and reprinted it. Because the adapter is a separate part, I can replace a worn one without rebuilding the whole wheel.

The Outcome

During bench testing, I got all three motor drivers working and measured about 0.3 A total for three unloaded motors. We presented the rover alongside the soil colorimeter in a joint presentation. Full-rover speed, range, battery runtime, and payload testing are the next steps.

If I built it again, I'd secure the wiring earlier, design a custom PCB to simplify the connections, and measure current under load before choosing the final battery and motor drivers. The biggest things I learned were how to wire and troubleshoot a multi-motor system, how to calibrate steering servos, and how to redesign a mechanical part after testing exposes a weakness.

Career Goals

My long-term goal is to work as an electrical engineer in the electric vehicle industry, at a company like Tesla, designing the power electronics, charging systems, and motor drives that make EVs work.

EVs bring together everything I enjoy about electrical engineering: high-power circuits, energy conversion, batteries, motors, and hardware that has to survive the real world. Why These Two Threads

Electric Energy Systems covers how power is generated, converted, and delivered, including the motor drives and power electronics at the heart of an EV. Circuit Technology covers the analog and digital circuits that control and protect those systems. Together they're the hardware skill set an EV engineer needs.

Right now I'm focused on getting real experience so I can see which areas of the industry I like and which I don't. My top priority is landing an engineering internship, ideally in EVs, power electronics, or automotive. Alongside that, I'm working on the EcoCAR team and building hands-on projects like my agricultural rover and USB-C charging module, which give me the practical hardware skills these jobs require.
